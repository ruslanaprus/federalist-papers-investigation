SET THE QUESTION BEFORE THE RESULTS

What are we trying to find out?

Does Federalist No. 49 resemble Hamilton's or Madison's writing more closely in its use of common grammatical words?

The essays argued for ratifying the United States Constitution under the shared name Publius. The Library of Congress lists No. 49 as “Hamilton or Madison.” That historical uncertainty motivates the question; it does not determine the answer.

Research approach

Primarily quantitative: count selected words and compare their frequencies. Add qualitative inspection: read examples to understand what the numerical differences mean.

A fixed design

Choose texts, words, preparation rules, comparison method, validation, and two reliability checks before calculating frequencies. Preserve the original plan and document changes separately.

Sources: Library of Congress authorship table · Dated research plan · Recorded amendments

MEASURE GRAMMATICAL HABITS
Why these 24 function words?

          
Function words commonly express grammatical relationships: of links nouns, and joins elements, and these points to something already mentioned. A feature is a measured property, here the frequency of one selected spelling.

          
Subject words such as army or tax can distinguish topics rather than writers. Common grammatical words offer a simple way to investigate recurring writing habits with less direct dependence on subject matter. Topic and context can still affect them.

          

          
This is a teaching selection fixed in advance, not a claim that these are the best authorship markers. Count each spelling regardless of its grammatical role. Keep function words in the analysis; do not apply a stopword filter.

          
Try the word-counting rule
Whole words follow [a-z]+(?:'[a-z]+)*. Internal apostrophes stay; hyphens split words. Numbers and punctuation are ignored. Word forms are not combined.

          
Reason for examining common words: Mosteller & Wallace (1963) · Fixed feature specification

MAKE DIFFERENT LENGTHS COMPARABLE
Count words, then calculate rates

          
A longer essay offers more opportunities for a word to occur. Convert raw counts to occurrences per 1,000 words, using all essay words as the denominator. Keep essays separate so each has equal weight in its author's profile.

          
frequency per 1,000 = selected-word count ÷ total essay words × 1,000

7
A TRANSPARENT COMPARISON
How do we measure resemblance?

          
  1. For each word, average the essay rates separately for Hamilton and Madison. Every essay gets equal weight.
  2. Calculate the word's standard deviation across the training essays together: how much the rates vary. Use sample scaling, dividing the squared deviations by n − 1 before taking the square root.
  3. Find the absolute difference between the test essay's rate and each author's mean. Divide both differences by the same training standard deviation.
  4. Average these standardized differences across usable words. The smaller distance indicates the closer profile.

          
word contribution = |test rate − author's mean| ÷ training standard deviation
author distance = average of the usable word contributions

          
Inspect all fitted word quantities for No. 49

          
A word with exactly zero training variation is omitted; none were omitted in these analyses. A distance gap at most 1e-12 is recorded as inconclusive. That tolerance handles numerical equality, not evidential strength. Distances are not probabilities.

TEST THE METHOD BEFORE NO. 49
Can it identify the known authors?

          
Leave-one-essay-out validation means temporarily treating one reference essay as unknown. Remove it completely, build profiles and scaling from the remaining 15, then compare it with both authors. Repeat for all 16 essays.

          
The held-out essay must not influence its own author averages or scaling. Otherwise, information about the answer enters the test: data leakage.

CONNECT NUMBERS TO LANGUAGE
What lies behind the scores?

          
For the main comparison, rank all words by the size of the difference between their Hamilton and Madison contributions. Inspect the top four: on, in, at, these. The first three favor Madison; these favors Hamilton. These four are for interpretation; the attribution still uses all 24 words.

          
All 24 feature contributions, ranked

          
Patterns that support the comparison
On occurs more often in every Madison reference than in any Hamilton reference, and that separation remains after quotation removal. No. 49's rate is closer to Madison's mean.
But “rest on” occurs in both No. 49 and Hamilton's No. 31. A shared expression is not an exclusive author marker.
Examples that complicate it
“In time of peace” occurs nine times in Hamilton's military discussion in No. 26, illustrating a possible topic effect. No. 49 uses at only in “at the expense of” and “at large.”
These favors Hamilton's average, although No. 49's rate is below every Hamilton reference. Closer to a mean does not mean typical of that author.

          
A concordance shows a word in its surrounding context. The saved collection contains all 1,414 occurrences of these four words, with 96 annotated examples, including all 53 occurrences in No. 49.

CHECK THE IMPLEMENTATION
Are the calculations trustworthy?

          
Recorded research verification checked word boundaries, apostrophes, equal essay weighting, sample scaling, ties, constant features, and separation of training and evaluation texts. An independent calculation checked the saved results.

          
The independent audit used a separate character scanner and 50-digit decimal arithmetic. It checked 1,156 count cells, 1,156 frequency cells, and 102 distances across the three versions. The largest numerical difference was below 1e-12. Repeated calculations agreed, and the saved evidence remained unchanged.
The browser replay is being checked against the saved results.

          
These checks support implementation correctness. They cannot establish historical authorship or accuracy on texts outside this small sample.

13
CONCLUDE IN PROPORTION TO THE EVIDENCE
A stable resemblance, with limits

          
Under the specified function-word method, Federalist No. 49 is closer to Madison's reference profile. The method correctly classified all 16 known-author essays, and both reliability checks retained the Madison assignment.

          
These results support a limited attribution within this comparison. They do not prove who wrote the essay or yield a probability of authorship.

          
What limits the conclusion?
  • Only eight reference essays per author, and only two candidate authors.
  • Topic effects, borrowed language, and editorial variation.
  • Some very close validation distances, particularly No. 78.
  • Supplied-text provenance for Nos. 21 and 26 and a quotation review made after the main result was known.
  • No independent, previously unseen final test corpus.
How does it compare with historical research?
Mosteller and Wallace's 1963 study used broader statistical analyses, including discriminant and Bayesian methods. It found evidence favoring Madison for all 12 disputed essays.
Our No. 49 result agrees in direction. This classroom experiment is much smaller and does not reproduce their statistical model or its evidential strength.
